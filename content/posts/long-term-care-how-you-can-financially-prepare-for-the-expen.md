Most families don't plan for long-term care. They react to it. A parent falls, a diagnosis changes everything, and suddenly a family is scrambling to pay $9,000 a month for a memory care facility with no plan, no policy, and no idea where the money is coming from. That reaction is expensive, and it's avoidable. Long-term care is a financial risk like disability or premature death: something you fund years before you need it, not after.

At Aurora Consulting Group, we sit down with clients across Visalia, Tulare County, Kings County, Fresno County, and Kern County who are running businesses, raising families, and quietly worried about what happens when a parent, or they themselves, need extended care. This is one of the most under-planned corners of personal finance, and it deserves the same rigor you'd apply to a business decision: know the real cost, compare your funding options, and build the plan into your broader tax and estate strategy before a crisis forces your hand.

## What long-term care actually costs

Cost-of-care surveys consistently show a wide range depending on the level of care and the setting. A home health aide providing part-time assistance might run $25 to $35 an hour. Assisted living facilities in Central California commonly charge $4,000 to $6,000 a month for a single resident. Skilled nursing care, the most intensive and expensive tier, often lands between $8,000 and $11,000 a month for a semi-private room, and more for private rooms or specialized memory care units.

Here's the number that catches most people off guard: the average length of a long-term care need is roughly three years. Multiply a mid-range monthly cost by 36 months and you're looking at a potential $300,000 exposure per person. Medicare does not cover this. Medicare pays for short-term rehabilitation after a hospital stay, not ongoing custodial care. Without a plan, that cost lands directly on savings, home equity, or a family's balance sheet.

## Three ways to fund the cost, and how they compare

There isn't one right answer here. The right funding mix depends on your age, health, assets, and whether you or a family member served in the military. Most solid plans blend more than one of these approaches.

| Funding method | Best fit | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance | Clients in their 50s to early 60s in good health who want to cap their exposure | Premiums rise with age and can increase after purchase; underwriting gets harder with health issues |
| Self-funding from savings and investments | Clients with $1.5 million or more in liquid assets who can absorb the cost without insurance | No cap on exposure; a long care need can erode a retirement portfolio faster than expected |
| VA Aid and Attendance benefit | Wartime veterans or surviving spouses meeting income and asset limits | Application process is detailed and can take months; benefit amount is fixed, not unlimited |

## Long-term care insurance versus self-funding

Long-term care insurance makes the most sense when you're still insurable and want to convert an open-ended risk into a fixed, budgetable expense. A policy purchased at 55 typically costs less and offers more flexible terms than one purchased at 65, assuming health hasn't changed. The trade-off is real: premiums are not guaranteed to stay flat, and insurers have raised rates on existing policyholders industry-wide over the past decade.

Self-funding works when the numbers support it. A client with a diversified portfolio, a paid-off home, and no major health red flags might reasonably decide the premiums aren't worth it if they can absorb a worst-case scenario without derailing retirement. This is exactly the kind of decision that belongs in a conversation with your CFO advisor rather than a solo Google search, because it depends on your full financial picture, not a generic rule of thumb. If you haven't stress-tested your portfolio against a scenario like this, our piece on [building a balanced investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) is a useful starting point before you make the insurance decision.

## The VA Aid and Attendance benefit for veterans and surviving spouses

If you or your spouse served during a wartime period, the [VA Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefit](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/) can offset a meaningful portion of long-term care costs. This is an additional monthly amount added to a veteran's pension for those who need help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, or managing medications, or who are largely confined to their home. It's income and asset tested, and the application requires documentation most families haven't gathered until they need it urgently.

We've walked clients in Tulare and Kern counties through this process, and the common thread is timing. Families who start the paperwork before a crisis get the benefit flowing faster than those applying while already paying out of pocket for care.

## Where long-term care planning intersects with your tax strategy

This is where a CPA firm adds value beyond a generic financial planning conversation. A portion of long-term care insurance premiums can qualify as a deductible medical expense, subject to age-based limits set by the IRS and the overall rule that medical expenses count only above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) lays out exactly how long-term care premiums and qualified long-term care services are treated for medical expense deduction purposes, and the details matter for how you structure the payment.

For business owners, there's another layer: depending on your entity structure, a portion of long-term care premiums may be deductible as a business expense rather than a personal itemized deduction, which changes the math significantly for S-corp owners and self-employed professionals. ## Common Questions

**How much does long-term care insurance typically cost?**
Premiums vary widely based on age, health, and coverage amount, but a healthy 55 year old might pay $1,500 to $3,000 a year for a policy with a meaningful daily benefit. Waiting until your mid-60s to purchase often doubles that cost, and health changes can make you uninsurable altogether.

**Can I deduct long-term care insurance premiums on my taxes?**
A portion of eligible long-term care insurance premiums can count toward your itemized medical expense deduction, subject to age-based caps and the 7.5% of adjusted gross income threshold detailed in [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502). Business owners should ask whether their entity structure allows the premium to be deducted as a business expense instead.

**Does the VA Aid and Attendance benefit cover the full cost of a nursing home?**
Usually not on its own. The benefit adds a fixed monthly amount to a veteran's or surviving spouse's pension and is meant to offset care costs, not replace insurance or savings entirely. Most families use it alongside self-funding or a long-term care policy.

**At what age should I start planning for long-term care?**
Most financial advisors recommend evaluating your options between ages 50 and 60, while you're still insurable and have time to build savings specifically earmarked for this purpose. Waiting until a health event forces the conversation removes insurance from the table entirely.
