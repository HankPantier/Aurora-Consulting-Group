A cabin in the Sierra foothills or a condo near the Central Coast can be a lifestyle purchase and a tax decision at the same time. The IRS doesn't care what you call the property on your mortgage application. It cares how many nights you slept there versus how many nights you rented it out, and that single number determines whether your second home functions as a tax shelter for personal enjoyment or gets treated as a rental business with its own set of rules, deductions, and reporting obligations.

For Central California buyers eyeing a lake house near Kern County or a mountain property outside Visalia, this distinction matters before you sign anything. Get the usage pattern right and you can deduct mortgage interest and property taxes like a second residence while still renting the place out part of the year, tax-free. Get it wrong and you could lose deductions you were counting on, or trigger passive activity loss limits that surprise you at filing time.

## The 14-day rule that decides everything

The IRS gives you a narrow but valuable exception: if you rent out your second home for 14 days or fewer during the year, you don't report the rental income at all. Not a dollar of it. You still get to deduct mortgage interest and property taxes as you would on any second residence, subject to the usual itemized deduction limits. This is sometimes called the "Augusta Rule" when applied to a primary residence, but it applies just as well to a vacation property.

The catch is that this exception only works one direction. The moment you exceed 14 rental days, all of that rental income becomes taxable, and you move into a different set of IRS categories entirely. IRS Publication 527 lays out the full mechanics of this threshold, and it's worth reading in full before you list your property on a short-term rental platform, even for a few weekends. [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527) covers rental of vacation homes specifically, including how to allocate expenses between personal and rental use.

## Second home vs investment property: why the label matters

A second home and an investment property are not interchangeable terms to the IRS, even though real estate listings use them loosely. The distinction comes down to personal use days relative to rental days, and it changes which deductions apply and how losses are treated.

| Classification | Personal use | Rental use | Tax treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Personal residence (14-day rule) | Any amount | 14 days or fewer | Rental income not reported; mortgage interest and property tax deductible as itemized deductions |
| Vacation home with mixed use | More than 14 days or more than 10% of rental days | More than 14 days | Expenses split between personal and rental use; rental losses often limited |
| Rental / investment property | 14 days or fewer, or 10% of rental days, whichever is greater | Substantial | Treated as a rental business; depreciation, full expense deductions, passive loss rules apply |

That middle category, the mixed-use vacation home, is where most Central California second-home owners actually land. If you spend three weeks a year at the property yourself and rent it out for two months during peak season, you're required to allocate expenses like utilities, insurance, and repairs between the personal-use days and the rental days. Only the rental-use portion of those costs becomes deductible against rental income, and if your personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days it was rented, you lose the ability to deduct a net rental loss against your other income. IRS Tax Topic 415 walks through this allocation method with worked examples. [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415) is the clearest starting point if you're trying to estimate your own split before the year is over.

## What happens once you cross into rental territory

Once your property clears the 14-day threshold and rental use dominates, the IRS treats it as a rental business on Schedule E. That opens the door to real advantages: depreciation deductions, full deductibility of property management fees, cleaning costs, and platform fees, and the ability to deduct a broader range of repair and maintenance expenses tied to the rental period.

It also opens the door to complications many second-home buyers don't anticipate. Depreciation recapture applies when you eventually sell. Passive activity loss rules under IRC Section 469 can limit how much of a rental loss you can use against W-2 or business income in a given year, depending on your adjusted gross income and whether you materially participate in managing the property. If the property sits in a different county or state from your primary residence, and many Central California second homes do (a lake property in Kings County while you live and work in Visalia, for example), you may also need to track whether that creates a state filing obligation depending on where the property sits relative to state lines.

## Common Questions

**What is the IRS 14-day rule for a second home?**
If you rent your second home for 14 days or fewer in a calendar year, you don't have to report any of that rental income on your tax return, and you can still deduct mortgage interest and property taxes as you would for a personal residence. Exceed 14 rental days and all of the income becomes taxable, with expenses allocated between personal and rental use.

**Is renting out a second home considered income?**
Yes, once you rent it for more than 14 days in a year. Below that threshold, the rental income is excluded entirely under the personal-use exception described in IRS Publication 527. Above it, the income is reportable on Schedule E and subject to expense allocation rules.

**How does the IRS decide if my property is a second home or an investment property?**
The classification depends on the ratio of personal-use days to rental days, not on what you call the property. If your personal use exceeds 14 days or 10% of the days it was rented, whichever is greater, it's treated as a vacation home with mixed personal and rental use rather than a straight investment property.

**Can I deduct losses on a vacation rental home?**
Sometimes, but the rules tighten as personal use increases. If personal use exceeds the 14-day or 10% threshold, net rental losses generally cannot offset other income and instead carry forward. Properties that qualify as primarily rental, with limited personal use, have more flexibility here, subject to passive activity loss limitations.
