A business owner in Visalia sells a rental property, rolls the proceeds into a brokerage account, picks a mix of funds, and calls it done. Eighteen months later, one tech stock has doubled and now makes up 40% of the account. The owner never decided to take on that much concentration risk. It happened while nobody was looking.

That's the trap with a balanced investment portfolio: balance is a moment in time, not a permanent state. Markets move, some holdings grow faster than others, and the allocation you built in January rarely resembles the one you're carrying by December. If you're not checking, you're not balanced anymore, no matter what the original plan said.

## Why the allocation you set last year isn't the one you have now

Asset allocation strategy starts with a simple question: how much of your money should sit in stocks, bonds, real estate, and cash given your goals and timeline? A 35-year-old professional saving for retirement can usually carry more equity risk than someone five years from selling a business and retiring on the proceeds. That's risk tolerance investing in practice, and it's a real calculation, not a gut feeling.

The problem is that percentages drift. If equities climb 20% while bonds stay flat, a 60/40 portfolio can quietly become 68/32 without a single trade. You're now carrying more risk than you agreed to, and you didn't sign up for it on purpose. The Federal Reserve's research on household finance and investment behavior points to exactly this pattern: households consistently under-monitor how allocations shift with market performance, which changes their actual risk exposure without any conscious decision on their part (see the [Federal Reserve's notes on household finance and investment behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm)).

## Portfolio diversification means more than owning different tickers

Owning ten mutual funds isn't diversification if eight of them hold the same twenty large-cap technology names. Real portfolio diversification spreads risk across asset classes, sectors, and geographies so that a downturn in one area doesn't take the whole account with it. For business owners, there's a second layer worth naming: your business itself is already a concentrated bet on one industry, one local market, one set of clients. A contractor in Kern County or a real estate broker in Tulare County has significant personal wealth tied up in a single local economy before a single investment dollar is deployed. That argues for more diversification in the investment account, not less, to offset the concentration already sitting in the business.

This is where tax planning and investment strategy start to overlap, and it's a conversation we have often with clients who run professional practices, agencies, and consulting firms. A CPA can't tell you which fund to buy, but a CPA absolutely should be looking at how your investment decisions interact with your tax return: capital gains timing, retirement account contribution limits, and the tax treatment of gains inside a business entity versus a personal brokerage account.

## The discipline of rebalancing, and why most people skip it

Portfolio rebalancing is the process of selling a bit of what's grown and buying a bit of what hasn't, to bring the account back to its target allocation. It sounds mechanical. It's actually uncomfortable, because it means selling your winners and buying more of whatever just underperformed. That discomfort is exactly why most investors avoid it, and exactly why skipping it quietly erodes returns over time.

A few approaches worth knowing:

| Rebalancing method | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Calendar-based | Rebalance on a fixed schedule, such as every January and July | Investors who want simplicity and predictability |
| Threshold-based | Rebalance only when an asset class drifts a set percentage from target, such as 5 points | Investors who want to limit unnecessary trading |
| Cash-flow rebalancing | Direct new contributions or withdrawals toward underweight assets | Business owners with irregular income who add or pull cash periodically |

For entrepreneurs whose income is lumpy, tied to commission cycles or seasonal client work, cash-flow rebalancing often makes the most practical sense. It avoids forcing a taxable sale just to hit a target percentage.

## Where taxes quietly decide whether rebalancing helps or hurts

Here's the piece that gets missed most often: rebalancing inside a taxable brokerage account can trigger capital gains. Sell an appreciated stock to bring your allocation back in line, and you may owe tax on that gain this year, even though your goal was risk management, not income. Rebalancing inside a 401(k) or IRA doesn't create that problem, since trades inside retirement accounts aren't taxable events.

That distinction changes the strategy. A multi-entity business owner with retirement accounts, brokerage accounts, and possibly a defined benefit plan tied to an S-corp has several buckets to rebalance across, and the smart move is often to rebalance inside tax-advantaged accounts first before touching taxable ones. ## Common Questions

**How often should I rebalance my portfolio?**
Most financial professionals recommend reviewing allocation at least twice a year, or whenever an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from its target. Business owners with irregular income often do better directing new contributions toward underweight assets rather than selling and triggering unnecessary tax events.

**Does rebalancing always create a tax bill?**
Only in taxable brokerage accounts. Rebalancing inside a 401(k), IRA, or other tax-advantaged account doesn't trigger capital gains, which is why coordinating across account types before making trades matters.

**Is a balanced investment portfolio the same for everyone at a similar age?**
No. Two 45-year-olds can need very different allocations depending on risk tolerance, whether they own a concentrated asset like a business or rental properties, and how close they are to needing the money. A business owner with significant wealth tied up in one company usually needs more diversification in their personal portfolio, not less.

**Can my CPA help with investment decisions, or is that only a financial advisor's job?**
A CPA typically won't select specific investments, but should absolutely be looped in on how gains, rebalancing, and account types interact with your tax return, entity structure, and estimated payments. That coordination is where a lot of avoidable tax liability gets caught.
