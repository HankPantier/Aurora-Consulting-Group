A dealership finance manager will tell you leasing means "lower payments" and buying means "you own something." Both statements are true and neither answers the question that actually matters: which option leaves more money in your pocket once taxes, depreciation, and your driving habits are factored in? At Aurora Consulting Group, we field this question constantly from real estate brokers driving 25,000 miles a year between showings, contractors hauling equipment to job sites across Tulare and Kings County, and consultants who put a vehicle on the books as a business expense. The right answer depends on specifics, not on what feels cheaper at the dealership.

## Start with your driving habits, not the sales pitch

Before any tax discussion, look at how the vehicle will actually be used. Leases typically cap mileage at 10,000 to 12,000 miles a year, with penalties running 15 to 30 cents per mile over that limit. A real estate broker showing properties across Fresno and Kern County can blow past 15,000 miles without trying. If that describes your routine, a lease that looked attractive at signing can cost an extra $1,000 or more by the time you turn in the keys.

Buying removes the mileage ceiling entirely. You keep the vehicle as long as it runs, and there's no dealership inspection at the end tallying up wear and tear. If your business depends on the truck or SUV staying useful for eight or ten years, ownership usually wins on this factor alone, before a single tax form comes into play.

## The tax math changes once the vehicle is used for business

Here's where the conversation shifts from personal finance to CPA territory. A vehicle used more than 50% of the time for business purposes opens the door to deductions that a purely personal car never sees. Leased vehicles let you deduct the business-use percentage of the monthly lease payment as an ordinary expense, which is simple to track but caps out at whatever the lease costs each month. Purchased vehicles, by contrast, can qualify for accelerated depreciation, including Section 179 expensing, which front-loads a much larger deduction into the year of purchase.

This is the detail dealership staff rarely explain, because it depends on your entity structure and income, not on the vehicle itself. A contractor operating as an S-corp with a strong profit year might benefit enormously from buying a heavy pickup and expensing a large chunk of the cost immediately. A consultant with a leaner year might prefer the steadier, smaller deduction a lease provides.

## Section 179 and depreciation rules you need to know before you sign

Section 179 allows businesses to deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment and vehicles in the year they're placed in service, rather than depreciating the cost over five or six years. Vehicles with a gross weight over 6,000 pounds, a category that includes many full-size SUVs and pickup trucks, qualify for a higher deduction limit than passenger cars. The specifics of what counts, how much you can deduct, and the phase-out rules for vehicles used less than 100% for business are laid out in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946), which governs how property is depreciated for tax purposes.

The catch: Section 179 only applies to purchased vehicles, not leased ones. If a large first-year deduction is the priority, that alone can tilt the decision toward buying, assuming your business has the profit to absorb the tax benefit and the cash flow to handle the purchase.

## Leasing versus buying by the numbers

A side-by-side comparison makes the trade-offs concrete. These are typical patterns, not universal rules, since actual figures depend on the vehicle, your credit, and your entity structure.

| Factor | Leasing | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly cost | Usually lower | Usually higher |
| Mileage limits | 10,000 to 12,000 miles/year, penalties apply | None |
| Upfront tax deduction | Business-use % of lease payment | Potential Section 179 deduction, up to full purchase price for qualifying vehicles |
| Long-term ownership | None, vehicle returned at lease end | Equity builds, vehicle is an asset |
| Best fit | Predictable low mileage, frequent vehicle upgrades | High mileage, long hold periods, strong profit year for tax benefit |

For a multi-entity business running vehicles across two or three LLCs, this table gets more complicated. Which entity should own or lease the vehicle, how mileage gets allocated between entities, and how the deduction flows through each entity's return are questions worth working out with an advisor before you sign anything, not after.

## Cash flow and financing realities right now

Financing costs matter as much as tax treatment. Auto loan rates move with broader credit conditions, and the [Federal Reserve's Consumer Credit (G.19) report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks how those rates and overall consumer borrowing trends shift over time. When rates run higher, the gap between a lease payment and a loan payment widens, which changes the monthly cash flow calculation even before you factor in taxes.

For a business owner already managing tight margins, a lower lease payment can free up cash for payroll, inventory, or marketing in a given quarter. For a business with steady profit and a use for a large first-year deduction, the higher monthly cost of a loan can be offset by the tax savings from depreciation. Neither approach is automatically smarter. The right call depends on what your cash flow statement and tax projection actually show, not on which payment number looks smaller on a dealership worksheet.

## Which niches see the biggest tax impact

The lease-versus-buy decision plays out differently depending on the business. [Real estate brokers](/industries/real-estate-brokers) driving high mileage between listings and closings tend to benefit from ownership, both for the deduction potential and the freedom from mileage caps. [Contractors](/industries/contractors) hauling tools and materials often need heavier trucks that qualify for larger Section 179 deductions, making purchase the more common choice once a truck exceeds the 6,000-pound threshold.

Consultants and other professionals with lower annual mileage, who value predictable expenses and like upgrading vehicles every few years, often lean toward leasing. Nonprofit organizations with vehicles used for outreach or client transport face their own set of rules around unrelated business income and depreciation that deserve a separate conversation with an advisor familiar with nonprofit accounting.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct a car lease payment on my business taxes?**
Yes, if the vehicle is used for business, you can deduct the business-use percentage of the monthly lease payment as an ordinary and necessary business expense. Personal use miles don't qualify, so accurate mileage tracking matters.

**Does Section 179 apply to leased vehicles?**
No. Section 179 expensing applies only to purchased vehicles that you own and place in service for business use. Leased vehicles are deducted through the lease payment method instead, as described in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946).

**Is it better to lease or buy a vehicle for a small business?**
It depends on mileage, profit level, and how long you plan to keep the vehicle. High-mileage users and businesses wanting a large first-year deduction tend to benefit from buying, while lower-mileage users who prefer predictable payments often do better leasing.

**What weight vehicles qualify for the higher Section 179 deduction?**
Vehicles with a gross vehicle weight rating over 6,000 pounds, a category that includes many full-size trucks and SUVs, qualify for a higher first-year deduction limit than standard passenger cars, subject to the rules in IRS Publication 946.
